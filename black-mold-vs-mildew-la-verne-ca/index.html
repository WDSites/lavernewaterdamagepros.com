Black Mold vs Mildew in La Verne, CA

Color is a poor way to sort growth in a house. Here is what you can read from the surface, and what points back to the water problem behind it, with options for mold remediation when it is needed.

Call (626) 879-0276

The answer first: you cannot sort them by color

Mildew is a casual word for surface mold, usually flat, powdery or fuzzy growth on a damp bathroom or window area. Black mold is a casual word for any dark growth, and it is not one single species. Many kinds of mold look black, green, gray, white or brown depending on the material and the stage of growth.

So a black spot is not automatically worse than a gray one, and a gray one is not automatically harmless. What matters for your house is the same in both cases: where the moisture is coming from, how big the area is, and what material the growth sits on. Questions about how any mold affects a person's health belong with a physician.

How the two usually show up

These patterns are common but not reliable. Treat the table as a description of what people see, not a way to name a species.

A house can have both at once, and different molds can grow side by side in the same patch.

Why color misleads people

Growth changes color as it matures. A patch that starts pale can darken as spores form, and the same mold can look different on tile than on wood. Soot, dirt, soap scum, dust and rust stains also pass for mold, and sometimes the reverse is true.

Because of this, reliable naming takes a lab. A lab can report what genus a sample contains, but the result still does not tell you whether the area needs to come out. That decision depends on the amount, the material and how wet the building was, which is why a crew measures moisture instead of guessing from the shade.

Follow the moisture, because that is the real tell

Every kind of mold needs water to grow. When you find growth, the useful question is not whether it is black but why that spot was wet. Look for a pattern that fits a source: a ring below a roof area, spotting along a cold exterior wall, growth behind a toilet or under a sink, or a musty smell from a closet near a wall with plumbing.

A moisture meter reading and a thermal camera scan let a crew separate surface condensation from a wet wall. If you have a leak, water damage repair and drying often come before any mold cleaning. Without that order, the growth tends to return because its water supply never stopped.

Clues that point to a moisture problem, not just a dirty surface

Any one of these deserves a closer look. Two or more together suggest a leak or ongoing dampness.

Look for these

  • Growth in a ring or a clear line below a roof, upstairs bath or water heater
  • Paint that bubbles, peels or feels soft under pressure
  • A musty odor that gets stronger when the room is closed up
  • Warped baseboards, cupped flooring or swollen cabinet bases
  • Growth that comes back after cleaning in the same spot
  • Wall surfaces that feel cool or damp to the touch

What testing can and cannot tell you

Surface and air samples

Tape lifts, swabs and air samples go to a lab. They can show whether elevated spore types are present compared with outdoor air. They cannot say whether a particular household member will react, and they cannot tell you the cause of the moisture.

Moisture mapping often matters more

Because mold needs water, finding and mapping wet materials is usually the most useful measurement. After remediation, some homeowners hire an independent tester for clearance sampling, which should be someone other than the company doing the work. Ask any company whether its crews work to the published ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, and ask whether California licenses mold work or whether only the contractor's general license applies.

Small growth you can handle, and when to stop

A small patch of mildew on tile, grout or a shower door can be cleaned with detergent and a brush, then dried well. Wear gloves, glasses and a mask, and ventilate the room. Do not use a stiff brush on drywall or ceilings, because scrubbing can loosen spores into the air.

Stop and call when the growth covers a large area, sits on soft or crumbling material, appears after a leak, or keeps returning. In those cases, the next step is stopping the water, drying the building materials, and then deciding what to remove. A crew near you in Pomona or Glendora works the same way as one in La Verne.

For insurance, sudden and accidental water damage is often covered while long-term leaks often are not. Check your policy, take dated photos, and call the adjuster before cleaning if leakage may have been going on for a while.

La Verne local note

Dry weather and indoor moisture in La Verne

A City of La Verne newsletter from February 2023 reported that the city was in moderate drought, with a 20% water-use reduction compared with 2020 and watering limited to one day a week. A dry year outside does not make a house dry inside, because bathrooms, laundry, plumbing and roofs create their own moisture.

The practical tie-back is irrigation. Even on a reduced watering schedule, sprinklers that spray stucco or hit foundation vents add water near the walls. Aim spray heads away from the house and check cold corners inside for spotting after a watering day.

Source: City of La Verne newsletter, February 2023 issue, citing the U.S. Drought Monitor (NIDIS) drought status for January 2023.
