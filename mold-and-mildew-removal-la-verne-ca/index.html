Mold and Mildew Removal in La Verne, CA

What you can clean, what you can dry, and what belongs in the trash depends on the surface. This guide goes material by material, with links to mold remediation for jobs that outgrow a sponge.

Call (626) 879-0276

Start with the material, not the color

Hard, nonporous surfaces such as glazed tile, glass, metal and sealed fixtures can usually be cleaned and kept. Porous surfaces such as drywall, ceiling tile, carpet pad, insulation and upholstered furniture absorb moisture and growth, so they are harder to clean and are often replaced once growth is established.

That one rule drives most decisions. Add two more: mold needs moisture, so drying comes before and after any cleaning, and a patch bigger than a few square feet or one that appeared after a leak should be assessed before you touch it. Questions about how mold affects a person belong with a physician.

Tile, grout and glass

Hard surfaces are the easy case. Scrub with dish detergent or a tile cleaner, rinse with clean water, and dry with a towel instead of letting the surface air-dry. Grout is porous, so it holds growth longer than the tile around it, and an old toothbrush or a stiff nylon brush gets into the lines.

If grout keeps darkening in weeks, the problem is usually dampness, not cleaning. Fix the ventilation, the caulk or a slow leak behind the tile. Never mix bleach with ammonia or acidic cleaners, because the fumes can be unsafe.

Painted drywall and wood

Painted drywall

A small dry spot with firm paint can be wiped with a barely damp cloth and detergent. Do not scrub hard or spray, and dry the area right away. If the wall is soft, stained in rings or dark patches keep coming back, the drywall is wet inside and should be assessed.

Wood

Finished wood trim cleans like tile if it is firm. Raw framing and subfloor are different, because mold can sit in the grain, and rot may be present. A crew can sand or clean some structural wood, and decide on replacement if it is soft.

Drywall and wood often tell different stories. Drywall fails faster, while wood can survive a lot more drying. A moisture meter helps you decide which one you actually have.

Fabric, carpet and soft goods

Washable clothes, curtains and bedding can usually be laundered and dried completely. Dry-clean-only items need a cleaner that handles water-damaged goods. Upholstered furniture and mattresses with visible growth are often not worth saving.

Carpet that stayed wet more than a day or two, or carpet pad of any age with growth, generally goes. Take photos and a list before discarding anything covered by a claim, since California's Department of Insurance advises waiting for the adjuster to inspect where possible.

What to throw away

Bag these items before carrying them through the house, and keep photos for your insurer.

Usually discard

  • Ceiling tiles and insulation with visible growth
  • Wet or growth-covered drywall, cut out in sections
  • Carpet pad and carpet that stayed wet for several days
  • Mattresses, cushions and stuffed items with growth
  • Paper, cardboard and books that stayed damp
  • Porous items with a lingering musty smell after washing

Doing the cleaning safely

Wear rubber gloves, safety glasses and an N95-style mask. Open a window or run a fan to the outside, and keep pets and kids out of the room. Use detergent and water, not sprays that mist the air, and bag debris before moving it.

If you plan to hire help, ask whether the company follows the published ANSI/IICRC S520 standard, ask whether California licenses mold work, and check the contractor's license on the state license board website. Mold and mildew removal questions

Mildew is a casual term for mold growing on a surface, usually flat and powdery. The word does not describe a different species. The same moisture control and cleaning rules apply.

Small, dry, firm spots can sometimes be cleaned and repainted. If the wall is soft, stained or keeps returning, the drywall is usually cut out. A moisture meter tells you which case you have.

Bleach works on hard surfaces but does not reach inside porous materials, and it adds water. Detergent and water is the usual first step. Never mix bleach with other cleaners.
